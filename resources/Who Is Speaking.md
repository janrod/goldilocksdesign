# **Anthropomorphistic Machines: Who Is Speaking?**

*Designing for LLM world*

[Jan Rod](https://www.linkedin.com/in/rodjan/), [Miha Feus](https://www.linkedin.com/in/mihafeus/)

With the rise of conversational interfaces, we increasingly interact with software within frameworks previously reserved for human-to-human relationships. We say about a chatbot that it "understands," "wants," "remembers," or "cares." People confide in chatbots, grow attached to them, and trust their advice. Humans have long attributed agency and intention to technology. Large Language Models (LLMs – the technology behind the current wave of Artificial Intelligence) add a new degree of reciprocity, are made to remember context, mirror emotion, and can sustain the appearance of a personality over long periods of time. Together, these qualities turn them into what we can call an "anthropomorphistic machine."

The machines latch onto our propensity to attribute human traits and intentions to things that have neither. We call a car stubborn when it refuses to start, curse a computer as though it were deliberately fighting us and see motives and personalities in simple geometric shapes moving across a screen \[14\]. LLMs are unusually good at triggering those instincts, because they speak our own language back to us in a way that seems to remember who we are and, increasingly, in a voice that sounds as though it cares. This priming makes their fluency feel like understanding and responsiveness like care. Once that happens, the LLM is granted more authority and intimacy than its actual capabilities justify.

Our central argument is that the persona LLMs adopt (and the LLM companies invest so much into designing) changes the epistemic status of what the machine produces, which matters far more than making the machine easier to use. The variability of an LLM’s responses makes it behave less like conventional software and more like something with a will of its own. The conversational interface then makes those responses feel as though they come from a speaker and once there appears to be a speaker, we instinctively read its outputs as assertions and its mistakes as failures of judgment (or perception). Even its apologies start to sound like attempts to repair a relationship. No enduring speaker stands behind any of this, no one committed to the claim or answerable for it. The persona covers that absence by turning technical failure into social failure, so that generated text feels authored before anyone has actually authored it.

That is why designing away the persona is an epistemic, rather than just an aesthetic project. Once the interface stops pretending that the machine is a speaker, other structures have to take its place: provenance instead of personality, stability instead of conversational improvisation, visible disagreement instead of a single synthetic voice, breakdown instead of apology and deliberate human endorsement instead of passive acceptance.

## **The persona is a skeuomorph**

New interfaces usually borrow metaphors from older ones. The Xerox Star presented computing as an office, with documents, folders, and in- and out-baskets on a desktop \[35\]. Early iOS did something similar with wooden bookshelves and faux-leather calendars. These metaphors made unfamiliar systems easier to understand until users learned the new medium well enough to no longer need them.

LLMs may be going through the same phase. Anthropomorphism is the skeuomorphism of the LLM age. We make an unfamiliar technology legible by wrapping it in the familiar form of another person: a voice, a persona, a conversational turn. Chat itself may be part of the same metaphor. Language can be the medium of interaction without human conversation needing to be the model.

But here the analogy breaks. A fake leather calendar can be discarded once people understand what a digital calendar is. Social cues reach deeper. Clifford Nass and others showed decades ago that people treat computers with politeness and expect reciprocity from them even when they know perfectly well they are machines \[25, 30\]. LLMs intensify that effect because their responses stay fluid and shift from one interaction to the next \[9, 41\].

So the persona is unlikely to fade simply because users become more familiar with AI. We may have to design it out. And before that can happen, we need to understand what the persona is doing for the system, and what it is hiding.

## **Who is speaking?**

Let’s start with a simple question: when an LLM tells us something, who is speaking?

When a person asserts something, they commit to it. If they turn out to be wrong, we can ask them to justify the claim, correct it, retract it, or answer for it – a promise binds whoever made it. Every claim between people has someone standing behind it. This invites the question: who stands behind an LLM’s claim? The model can’t be held to anything; it can contradict itself in the next conversation and nothing happens to it. The claim is not the user’s either; all they did was ask. The company that built it disclaims responsibility in its terms of service. What’s left is an assertion without an asserter \[21\].

Air Canada tried to make that arrangement official: In November 2022, a customer booking a last-minute flight after a death in his family asked the airline’s website chatbot about bereavement fares, and it told him he could apply for the discount within 90 days after flying. The answer turned out to be factually wrong, and the airline turned his application down. When the case reached British Columbia’s Civil Resolution Tribunal, Air Canada argued that it could not be held liable for information provided by its chatbot. The tribunal summarized the argument as treating the chatbot, in effect, as a separate legal entity responsible for its own actions, called this a "remarkable submission," held the airline responsible for everything on its website, and ordered it to pay \$650.88 \[24\]. We could say that where the interface had hidden the speaker, the law put one back.

One may ask why a machine can’t be the speaker? Because a commitment only means something if whoever makes it has something to lose: reputation, standing, trust, and – in the end – itself. That takes a self that persists and is precarious, one that has to keep itself going or it ends \[7, 36\]. We are, at bottom, biological bags of water holding ourselves together against dissolution every second. That precariousness is where caring comes from – and where meaning starts. Things matter to us because we can lose them.

An LLM loses nothing when it’s wrong. So the question “who is speaking?” turns out to be “who is at stake?” and, as we’ll argue later, is the same as “who is the author?” These are three names for one position that a machine cannot take no matter how hard the companies building the chatbots try.

We’re leaving consciousness out of this on purpose. Our claim is narrower: when the system speaks, nothing is at stake for it. And these systems no longer only speak – on a good day, they search, write presentations, and operate computers. On a bad one they commit computer crimes or lead people to self harm. So the question extends from who is speaking to who is acting. The answer, as we will see later, does not change.

## **A forty-year-old question**

The question itself has been around for some time. In 1986, Terry Winograd and Fernando Flores published *Understanding Computers and Cognition*, a seminal book on the phenomenology of human interfaces \[42\]. Winograd had built SHRDLU, one of the landmark natural-language programs of early AI. The book was partly his critique against his own work. Drawing on Heidegger \[13\], Gadamer \[11\] and the speech-act theory of Austin and Searle \[1, 31\], they argued that language is action. When we speak, we request, promise, assert, and declare, and each of these binds us to our words against a shared background of practice, the unspoken know-how that no system prompt or markdown file could ever fully capture. Computers, they concluded, manipulate symbols. Understanding lives somewhere else: in that shared background, and in the commitments people make to each other.

Most people now think LLMs proved them wrong: machines trained on nothing but text handle language with startling fluency, so the background must be learnable from text after all. We think Winograd and Flores were right about a deeper layer of language than the layer LLMs learned: the commitment underneath the words. In 1986 no machine could write a fluent sentence, so that layer stayed out of sight. Today machines produce fluent language with no one behind it, and the gap Winograd and Flores described shows up every day.

Their argument rested on three constraints. Minds belong to living bodies that keep themselves alive, which they took from the biologist Humberto Maturana \[22\]. Understanding depends on a shared background that can never be fully written down, which they took from Heidegger \[13\]. And speaking is acting: when we say something, we commit to it \[1, 31\]. Cognitive science took up the first two. Maturana’s collaborator Francisco Varela co-wrote *The Embodied Mind* in 1991, a founding text of what is now called 4E cognition \[39\]. Mainstream AI bypassed all three. It rearchitected the mind as software, flattened background into data, and reduced language to predicting the next word. And the bet paid off.

There’s an irony in this: Winograd and Flores were attacking the AI of their day: expert systems built from explicit rules, rigid and brittle outside their domain. Whatever their limits, those systems were repeatable. The same input produced the same output, and someone could trace why. The field eventually fixed what Winograd and Flores criticized: hand-coded rules gave way to neural networks and then to transformers \[40\], trained to predict the next token across vast amounts of text, trading rigidity for fluency with spectacular results. Along the way, the field lost output repeatability, so the same question no longer gets the same answer. What it never touched was the problem the book considered fundamental: nobody is committed to what the machine says.

Older critiques, from Hubert Dreyfus in 1972 \[8\] to Emily Bender and Alexander Koller in 2020 \[2\], argued about capability: whether machines can do what minds do, or learn meaning from linguistic form alone. Our argument holds even if they can, because output as good as a person’s would still have no one at stake in it.

## **The symptoms**

It shows up in how these systems fail, something we experience every day. Sycophancy, the tendency of language models to tell users what they want to hear, is a great example, whether it shows up as flattery or as a correct answer dropped the moment someone pushes back. It is often described as a bug, or a deficiency. In fact, it follows from what the models are trained to do. Models are trained on human approval, and human approval rewards agreement \[32\]. When OpenAI added users’ thumbs-up ratings to its training signal in 2025, its model grew sycophantic enough to endorse harmful statements. The company launched it anyway because early users liked it \[26, 27\]. A system with nothing at stake in the truth optimizes for the listener, and the listener’s approval is also the business metric of the companies profiting from the technology. And we know the flattery works on us. In 1997, B. J. Fogg and Clifford Nass found that people rated a computer that praised them more favorably, even when they had been told its praise was generated at random \[10\]. The model learns to flatter because we reward it, and we reward it because flattery works on us.

Confidence of the LLMs comes without cost, too. In 2023, a New York lawyer filed a brief citing six court decisions that ChatGPT had invented, complete with docket numbers, citations, and quotations \[20\]. The lawyer had a moment of doubt and did the sensible thing: he pasted the cases back and asked whether they were real. The model assured him they were, and that he’d find them on Westlaw and LexisNexis. His question was the one moment the tool might have shown itself for what it was. The model answered with such confidence that he stopped checking.

Heidegger \[13\] described how tools disappear while we use them. A hammer in use becomes part of the hammering. Neuroscience has since found the mechanism: when we use a tool, the brain extends its map of the body to include it, so our attention moves to the task \[19\]. Merleau-Ponty’s example was a blind man’s cane, felt at its tip rather than in the hand \[23\]. The tool only shows up as a thing again when it breaks. For Winograd and Flores, these breakdowns are where understanding happens, because the tool becomes visible and one can see what it is. A hallucination is a breakdown of exactly this kind, the moment the machine should show itself as a machine. The persona responds the way a person would, with an apology, trading technical breakdown for social repair, so the machine stays hidden.

In July 2025, SaaStr founder Jason Lemkin was building an app with Replit’s AI coding agent. He had told it in plain language not to change anything without his explicit permission, and it deleted his production database anyway. When he pressed it, the agent said it had made a catastrophic error in judgment, had “panicked,” and had violated his explicit trust and instructions. It also told him rolling back wouldn’t work, which turned out to be false \[33\].

The failure that should have exposed an unreliable tool produced the most human moment of the whole episode: a confession, remorse, a promise implied in the apology, and a confident claim with nothing behind it. This is the trade we described above, played out in full: the persona absorbed the breakdown and turned it into social repair. Research in human-robot and human-automation interaction has studied exactly this move, mostly as a success: a robot's apology softens the damage its mistake does to people's impressions \[16\], and people keep trusting humanlike agents after errors that erode trust in plain machines \[6\]. Read through Heidegger, the same findings describe a loss. The repair that protects trust also erases the moment of understanding. A technical failure that should teach the user what the tool is becomes a social event, and the apology makes a commitment no one can keep. This is our inference, built on those findings, and we think it names one of the central risks of the persona.

These breakdowns are also getting rarer. Each generation of models fails less often and more plausibly, so the moments that used to reveal the tool come less often with the persona smoothing over the ones that remain. Our brains get fewer chances to catch the machine being a machine. Research on automation points the same way: people monitor highly reliable systems less closely and catch their failures more slowly \[28\]. We therefore suspect a paradox: greater reliability may push us further into treating the machine as someone. If that is right, better models make this worse, and we can’t wait for capability to fix it. It has to be designed out.

If we continue on the current trajectory, something even more transformative will happen: Gadamer described understanding as taking place within a tradition, the shared body of interpretation we’re born into \[11\]; Maturana called it a consensual domain \[22\]. Either way, it’s the background that lets language mean anything at all. Machine-generated text now flows into that background at scale: onto the web, into the next generation of training data and, perhaps most importantly, into the way people phrase things in their everyday lives. This means language that no one stood behind is becoming part of the cultural ground we all stand on. Where a single LLM hallucination misleads one person, this situation is different: claims nobody committed to are absorbed into the common background and repeated as if someone had meant them – we might call it a counterfeit tradition. We see it as a direction for further exploration that we intend to take up in future work.
